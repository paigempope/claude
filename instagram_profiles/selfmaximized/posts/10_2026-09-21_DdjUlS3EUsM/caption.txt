WHY EMOTIONS MOVE THROUGH US LIKE WAVES. THE SCIENCE IS BEAUTIFUL:
We reach for the wave metaphor instinctively when describing emotion. Grief arrives in waves. Joy washes over us. Anger rises and recedes. It turns out this is not just poetic language. It is a remarkably accurate description of what is actually happening inside the body and brain.

Emotions are not static states that switch on and off. They are dynamic physiological events with a beginning, a peak, and an end. Research by neuroscientist Jill Bolte Taylor suggests that the chemical processes behind an emotional response last approximately ninety seconds. What we experience as a prolonged emotional state is often the same wave being retriggered, again and again, by thought and memory. The ocean does not stop. We keep calling it back.

The autonomic nervous system moves in rhythms too. The constant oscillation between sympathetic activation and parasympathetic rest is itself wave-like, a biological tide that the body rides continuously. When emotion surges, the nervous system responds with a cascade of hormones, cortisol, adrenaline, oxytocin, that rise, peak, and naturally begin to metabolize. The body is designed to move through emotion, not hold it indefinitely.

Research on grief specifically, by Stroebe and Schut, describes what they call the oscillation model, the way bereaved people naturally move between confronting loss and stepping away from it. Back and forth. Into the wave and out again. This rhythm, they found, is not avoidance. It is how the human system processes something too large to hold all at once.

The wave analogy also carries its most important truth in what it implies about impermanence. Waves do not last. They cannot. The physics of a wave requires that it rise, crest, and fall. So does the physics of feeling.

You do not have to survive the whole ocean. Just the wave you are in right now.
