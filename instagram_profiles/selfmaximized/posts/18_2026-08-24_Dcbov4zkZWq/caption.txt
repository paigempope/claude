DOGS AND MENTAL HEALTH - THE SCIENCE IS REMARKABLE:
There is something that happens in the presence of a dog that is difficult to fake and impossible to manufacture. The greeting that requires nothing of you. The warmth of a body settling beside yours. The complete, uncomplicated quality of their attention. Most people who have loved a dog already know what it does to them. The science is now explaining why.

Interacting with a dog, even briefly, triggers a measurable release of oxytocin, serotonin, and dopamine in the human brain. Cortisol drops. Blood pressure lowers. Heart rate slows. These are not subtle effects. They are the same neurochemical shifts produced by meditation, physical affection, and meaningful social connection. The body responds to a dog the way it responds to safety.

Research consistently shows that dog owners have lower rates of depression, anxiety, and loneliness than non-owners. They report higher levels of self-esteem, greater sense of purpose, and stronger social connection. Dogs create routine, which the nervous system depends on. They create reasons to move, to be outside, and to engage with the world on days when the pull toward isolation is strongest.

The therapeutic use of dogs in clinical settings has expanded significantly as the evidence base has grown. Dogs are now used in the treatment of PTSD, depression, autism, dementia, and chronic pain. A single session of animal assisted therapy has been shown to reduce anxiety and cortisol in ways that persist beyond the session itself.

There is also the quality of love a dog offers that has no human equivalent. It is not conditional on your mood, your output, your appearance, or your history. It is simply there, consistent and whole, every single time.

For a nervous system that has spent years navigating conditional acceptance, that kind of love is genuinely therapeutic.
